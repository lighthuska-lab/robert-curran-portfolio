SHEET 04 — NOTES & LEGEND

HOW I WORK

I’m Robert Curran. I’ve spent a decade working on the data paths behind search, supply chain systems, and foundation models. My focus is how those systems perform—and how they behave when the input or a processing stage goes wrong.

01 —The systems years

I started in search and discovery at Amazon after studying Computer Science at SUNY Oswego. The work was concrete: return product results faster and keep the index current. Query batching, caching, and parallel retrieval reduced p95 latency by 35%; asynchronous incremental updates reduced index refresh time by 72%.

The request path was only part of the work. Indexing consumers also needed retries and failed-message reprocessing. A fast response depended on data that had made it through a reliable update path. That connection between performance and recovery runs through the systems I have worked on since.

02 —When the data has to arrive

In supply chain engineering, the focus moved to inventory and demand data. I worked on synchronization services that brought downstream update latency from 12 minutes to 90 seconds, and Spark pipelines whose daily processing time fell from 4.5 hours to 1.2 hours.

Those numbers sit alongside less visible work: deduplication, validation, reconciliation, exception routing, and replay. Repeated events and downstream failures were cases to handle in the implementation and exercise in tests. The system needed to make progress and have a way to recover when processing was interrupted.

03 —From pipelines to foundation models

My ML infrastructure work carries that same concern for the data path into training. For Nova, I worked with text, images, and video: preparing records, filtering invalid input, prefetching batches, and overlapping loading with GPU execution.

The improvements were measured separately. Data pipeline throughput increased by 40%. Training tokens per second increased by 32% with the same model and hardware. Data-related failures fell by 35%. Each result describes a different part of the system, and together they explain why the pipeline mattered.

Customization added another question: what did the new checkpoint improve, and what did it lose? My HyperPod work connected data conversion and mixing with CPT and SFT recipes, then compared customized checkpoints with their starting models on domain tasks and general instruction-following.

04 —How I work with a team

My Nova pipeline responsibility extended from design through rollout, in coordination with research and infrastructure teams. The implementation included data preparation and loading, while the collaboration connected that work to the training workflow it served.

Across my roles, the recurring work has been making the system understandable at its boundaries: validating records before processing, standardizing paths and configuration between jobs, monitoring stages, and testing failures before release. Those are the details I bring to discussions about throughput, reliability, and what is ready to ship.

05 —A common thread

I’m based in Selden, New York. My background is in Computer Science, and my engineering work spans backend services, data processing, and ML infrastructure.

The projects have changed over time, but the thread is consistent: dependable inputs, useful measurements, and systems that can recover. The case studies show the implementation work; the résumé puts it in the context of my career.
